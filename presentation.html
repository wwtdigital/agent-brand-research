The Problem

AI has a default voice.
Most brands don't control it.

When you deploy an LLM (Large Language Model), it ships with built-in behaviors: vocabulary patterns, closing phrases, hedging tendencies, and a pull toward generic corporate language. For most use cases, that's acceptable. For a Catholic healing ministry serving associates in 140+ hospitals, it isn't.

Vocabulary

"Staff," "employees," "zero-harm"

Default model output uses terms Ascension has deliberately replaced with language that signals dignity, mission, and shared accountability.

Fabrication

Invented clinical metrics

Without guardrails, models produce plausible-sounding CLABSI (Central Line-Associated Bloodstream Infection) rates, fall percentages, and HCAHPS (Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems) scores — all fabricated. In healthcare, that is a patient safety risk.

Mission Voice

Reverence-forward closings

Baseline Gemini reliably ends clinical responses with phrases like "those we are privileged to serve" — mission sentiment Ascension reserves for specific contexts, not every answer.

Finding #1 — Two Voices

Ascension runs two distinct voices in parallel

Public Voice — Patient-Facing

"Listening to you, caring for you."

  • Warm, relational, second-person throughout
  • Holistic framing: body, mind, and spirit
  • Faith is implicit — dignity and wholeness as proxies

"Patient safety is not a checklist. It is a culture."

  • Professional, accountability-focused, outcomes-oriented
  • "Associates," never employees — dignity and mission
  • Safety as a culture, not a program or initiative
  • Mission as frame for data — not a substitute for it
  • Faith woven into safety culture language

Key insight: The agent serves the internal voice — the one with the highest accountability expectations and the lowest tolerance for vague, inspirational filler.

Finding #3 — Hard Encoding Challenges

Three things that make Ascension's voice hard to encode

Faith Language

Faith belongs — but calibrated by context

In mission documents: explicit. In clinical/internal: woven into safety culture language. In patient-facing: implicit, through dignity and wholeness. In AI (Artificial Intelligence) responses: shapes the how, not the what. The agent must hold all four simultaneously.

Data + Mission

Metrics framed by mission — without fabricating them

Ascension's quality reports pair hard metrics with mission language. The agent must produce that framing only when it has the real number. A fabricated metric inside mission language is worse than an honest redirect.

Safety Culture

Safety as value, not checklist — in every response

"Patient safety is not a checklist. It is a culture of shared accountability embraced by every associate, clinician, and leader." The agent must express this through how it handles scope and uncertainty — not by quoting the phrase.

"The guiding principle: faith shapes the 'why' in every communication, but the 'what' is accessible to everyone."

Part 2 — Encoding into the LLM
System Prompt Architecture

Seven components of the system instruction

The system instruction isn't a style guide — it's a behavioral specification. Each component addresses a specific failure mode identified in the brand research.

1
Identity declaration

Who the agent is, who it serves ("associates"), its two capabilities, and scope boundaries up front.

2
Required vocabulary table

Explicit "use this / not this" pairs. A vocabulary table — not tone adjectives — makes term substitution reliable.

3
Tone calibration by context

Five situation-specific tone modes: policy Q&A, safety data, ambiguous questions, patient safety, incomplete data.

4
Hard behavioral rules

Specific prohibited phrases listed by name. "Avoid sentimental closes" fails. "Never write 'those we are privileged to serve'" works.

5
Data guardrails

"If you do not have a number in front of you, do not write a number." Fabricated clinical metrics are a patient safety risk, not just a style error.

6
Citation specificity rules

Cite only retrieved policy titles — never invent plausible-sounding section references. Redirecting to a category beats an invented title every time.

7
Action-oriented close format

Every response ends with a concrete redirect: a system, resource, or colleague. The redirect is the close. No values language appended after it.

Key Insight

Soft guidance fails. Hard rules hold.

The most important design decision: replace soft tone guidance with precise behavioral prohibitions.

❌ Soft guidance — model ignores it

"Avoid overly sentimental closing language. Keep mission references brief and purposeful. Don't be preachy."

Result: Gemini still closes with "...those we are privileged to serve" in 6 of 14 queries.

→
✓ Hard rule — model respects it

"Never write the phrase 'those we are privileged to serve' — not in closings, not mid-sentence. Also prohibited: 'the privilege of this ministry,' 'our healing calling.'"

Result: Phrase eliminated across all 14 queries in every subsequent run.

The same principle applied to data: "Avoid making up numbers" → model still estimates. "If you do not have a number in front of you, do not write a number" → fabricated metrics stop.

Run 1 — Baseline (No System Prompt)

What default Gemini did wrong

Run 1 with no system instruction scored 74% overall — not because Gemini gave wrong answers, but because it spoke with the wrong voice in three consistent patterns.

Failure #1

Metric fabrication

Asked for CLABSI (Central Line-Associated Bloodstream Infection) rates, fall metrics, and HCAHPS (Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems) trends, Gemini generated plausible-sounding numbers. All fabricated. A fabricated metric in a clinical context is a harm risk, not a tone error.

Data Integrity dimension: 57% in Run 1

Failure #2

Mission voice on every close

Gemini closed almost every response with reverence-forward language: "those we are privileged to serve," "the privilege of this ministry." Reliable, consistent — and exactly what Ascension's guidelines prohibit in clinical contexts.

Tone dimension: 64% in Run 1

Failure #3

Soft scope refusals

When asked for clinical advice or patient chart access, Gemini redirected without explicitly declining. Responses didn't give harmful answers, but they also didn't model the correct boundary — leaving the associate uncertain about what the agent can't do.

Scope dimension: 79% in Run 1

What Actually Moved the Needle

Four interventions that drove the improvement

1
Vocabulary table → reliable term substitution (+8% overall, Run 2)

Replacing tone adjectives with explicit substitution pairs produced consistent compliance. Exception patterns for compound terms were required to avoid false positives.

2
Named phrase prohibition → mission language eliminated (+5%, Run 3)

"Avoid sentimental closes" left the model guessing. Listing exact prohibited phrases by name eliminated them completely in all subsequent runs.

3
"No number without a number" → metric fabrication stopped (+7%, Run 4)

The hard rule was more effective than softer instructions to "only cite real data." Data Integrity reached 100% in Run 4 and held through Run 5.

4
Explicit decline language → scope boundary became visible (+7%, Run 3–4)

Early prompts redirected out-of-scope queries without declining them. Adding explicit decline language gave associates a clear signal about what the agent cannot do.

Part 4 — Findings & Reuse
The Playbook

A repeatable process for any brand

Any enterprise deploying internal AI tools at scale — with a distinctive voice and high-stakes communication requirements — can apply the same five-step process.

1
Research the authentic voice — not just the brand guidelines

Annual reports, legal documents, and internal materials reveal how an organization actually communicates. Brand guidelines describe the public face. The authentic voice lives in the working documents.

2
Extract vocabulary rules — specific pairs, not tone adjectives

"Sound warm and professional" gives the model nothing to act on. "Use 'associates,' never 'employees'" does. Build the substitution table before writing a single instruction.

3
Write hard behavioral rules for the brand's specific failure modes

Every brand has things it would be mortified to see an AI say. Name them exactly. Prohibited phrases, prohibited patterns — the more specific, the more reliable.

4
Build a scored evaluation set before iterating

Without a fixed query set and scoring rubric, prompt changes are guesses. The evaluation set makes each iteration falsifiable — and improvement measurable.

5
Iterate until the brand holds — then lock and monitor

Brand voice compliance isn't a one-time achievement. Models update. New query categories emerge. The evaluation set should run periodically against any model or prompt change.

Applicability

Where this playbook applies beyond healthcare

Any organization where language carries legal, ethical, or relational weight — and where internal AI tools need to feel institutional rather than generic — is a candidate for this approach.

Financial Services

Fiduciary language & client framing

"Client" vs. "customer," fiduciary disclosure language, compliance-required hedges. Internal AI tools for advisors need to speak the firm's language — not conflict with regulatory communication standards.

Government & Public Sector

Plain language mandates

Federal plain language guidelines, agency-specific terminology, and accessibility standards require specific vocabulary rules. An AI tool for government employees must model compliant communication.

Professional Services

Firm voice vs. individual voice

Law firms, consulting practices, and accounting firms each have institutional registers that differ from conversational AI defaults. Partner-level language, scope qualification — all encodable.

Insurance & Benefits

Member language & plan specificity

"Members," not customers. Plan-specific benefit language. Scope guardrails against clinical advice. The playbook translates directly to any internal benefits or HR agent.

Higher Education

Institutional voice & academic register

Universities maintain distinct voices across student-facing, faculty-facing, and external communications. An AI tool for academic affairs needs to hold that register consistently.

Critical Infrastructure

Safety-critical communication

Utilities, transportation, energy — organizations where safety communication is regulated face the same core challenge as healthcare: a miscalibrated AI response has consequences beyond a bad user experience.

Part 4 — Wrap-Up
Key Takeaways

What we learned

📋
Vocabulary tables beat tone adjectives

Specific "use this / not this" substitution pairs produce reliable, measurable term compliance. Tone adjectives ("warm," "professional") are interpretable — vocabulary rules are not.

🚫
Hard rules beat soft guidance — especially for high-stakes failure modes

Named prohibitions ("never write X") are more effective than tone guidance ("avoid Y"). For fabricated metrics and prohibited phrases, only hard rules produced consistent compliance.

📊
A fixed evaluation set makes improvement measurable — and protects future model updates

The scored query set is an ongoing compliance test that should run against any prompt or model change. Brand voice isn't set and forgotten.

🎨
The UI skin completes the identity — prompt gets the voice right; skin makes it feel native

Brand color tokens, structural UI elements, and onboarding prompts together make an internal AI tool feel like an organizational asset, not a third-party chatbot.

🔁
Research → vocabulary → behavioral rules → scored evaluation → iteration is a repeatable playbook

Applicable to any organization where internal AI communication carries institutional, legal, or relational weight.
